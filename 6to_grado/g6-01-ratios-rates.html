Part 1 · Explain

Athena makes lemonade with 3 scoops of mix for every 4 cups of water. Elías makes his with 5 scoops for every 7 cups of water. Elías says: “They taste the same, because in both we use one more cup of water than scoops.” Is he right? Whose lemonade is stronger?

Athena prepara limonada con 3 cucharadas de polvo por cada 4 tazas de agua. Elías prepara la suya con 5 cucharadas por cada 7 tazas de agua. Elías dice: «Saben igual, porque en las dos usamos una taza de agua más que cucharadas». ¿Tiene razón? ¿Qué limonada es más fuerte?

Sentence starters: Elías is ___ because ___. If both use 28 cups of water, Athena needs ___ scoops and Elías needs ___ scoops. So ___’s lemonade is stronger.

Useful words: ratio, equivalent, for every, ratio table, compare, stronger, scoops per cup.

Part 2 · Apply the idea to a new problem

The school store sells 4 notebooks for $5. Sophia has $20. She says: “I can buy 16 notebooks.” Is she right? Explain with a ratio table or a unit rate, and then write your own problem like this one.

La tienda de la escuela vende 4 cuadernos por $5. Sophia tiene $20. Ella dice: «Puedo comprar 16 cuadernos». ¿Tiene razón? Explica con una tabla de razones o una tasa unitaria y después escribe tu propio problema parecido.

Sentence starters: Sophia is ___ because $20 is ___ times $5, so ___. The price for 1 notebook is ___.

Challenge: another store sells 6 notebooks for $8. Which store has the better price per notebook? Show both unit rates.

Check your own work

Read your answers again. Press each sentence that is true about your work.

Vuelve a leer tus respuestas. Pulsa cada oración que sea verdadera sobre tu trabajo.

Part 1. Elías is not right. Having “one more cup of water” does not make two ratios equivalent; equivalent ratios come from multiplying both quantities by the same number. To compare, I use the same amount of water, 28 cups: Athena’s 3 : 4 is the same as 21 : 28 (× 7), and Elías’s 5 : 7 is the same as 20 : 28 (× 4). With the same water, Athena uses 21 scoops and Elías uses 20, so Athena’s lemonade is stronger.

Part 2. Sophia is right. $20 is 4 times $5, so she can buy 4 times as many notebooks: 4 × 4 = 16. With a unit rate: $5 ÷ 4 = $1.25 per notebook, and 16 × $1.25 = $20.

Challenge. For 10 cups of water Athena needs 7.5 scoops: 3 : 4 is the same as 0.75 : 1, and 0.75 × 10 = 7.5 (or 6 : 8 plus 1.5 : 2). The second store charges $8 ÷ 6 ≈ $1.33 per notebook, and the school store charges $1.25, so the school store has the better price.

Your words can be different. What matters is that your answer shows the math and explains how it proves your answer.

Aligned to the Illinois sixth grade mathematics standards 6.RP.1 (understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language to describe a ratio relationship between two quantities), 6.RP.2 (understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a : b with b ≠ 0, and use rate language in the context of a ratio relationship) and 6.RP.3 (use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems: a) make tables of equivalent ratios, find missing values and plot the pairs on the coordinate plane; b) solve unit rate problems, including unit pricing and constant speed; c) find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100 and find the whole given a part and the percent; d) use ratio reasoning to convert measurement units). Every ratio comes with a situation. At the Easy level the groups are drawn, the ratio table shows the multiplier for each row, the double number line uses equal jumps and the percent appears on a 10 × 10 grid or a tape in tenths; at the Challenge level the child counts a mixed collection, finds missing values through the unit rate, reads graphs of ratio tables, compares best buys, converts units, finds the whole from a part, and in “Who is right?” must also choose the reason. The most common mistake, adding the same amount to both quantities instead of multiplying, gets its own hint. El error más común, sumar la misma cantidad a las dos partes en lugar de multiplicar, tiene su propia pista.
