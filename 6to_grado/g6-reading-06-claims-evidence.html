The essay: Video Games Aren't All Bad, by Daniel

1Many adults think video games are a waste of time. I think video games, played for a limited time, can actually be good for kids.

2First, some games can sharpen your brain. Scientists who study action games have found that players can get better at noticing details and at switching their attention quickly.

3Second, many games teach teamwork and planning. In building games, players must plan, gather materials and solve problems, and in team games they have to communicate to win. My cousin and I built a whole working city together in one game, and we had to agree on every street.

4Of course, too much gaming can be a problem. Screens late at night can make it harder to fall asleep, and hours of sitting are not healthy. That is why kids should play for a limited time, like one hour a day, and never right before bed.

5Video games make kids smarter than kids who read books. Used wisely, games can be one more way to learn.

Part 1 · Judge the argument

What is Daniel's claim? Find one claim in the essay that is supported by evidence and one that is not supported. Quote both, and explain how you know.

¿Cuál es la afirmación de Daniel? Busca en el ensayo una afirmación apoyada con evidencia y otra sin apoyo. Cita las dos y explica cómo lo sabes.

Sentence starters: Daniel's claim is ___. The claim “___” is supported, because ___. The claim “___” is not supported, because ___.

Useful words: claim, reason, evidence, study, example, anecdote, supported, unsupported, counterclaim.

Challenge: explain how Daniel answers the counterclaim in paragraph 4, and say whether his answer makes his argument stronger.

Reto: explica cómo responde Daniel al contraargumento del párrafo 4 y di si su respuesta hace más fuerte su argumento.

Part 2 · Your own argument

Write a short argument (five or six sentences) about a change you would like at your school or in your neighborhood. Include a claim, two reasons, at least one piece of evidence and an answer to someone who might disagree.

Escribe un argumento corto (cinco o seis oraciones) sobre un cambio que te gustaría en tu escuela o en tu barrio. Incluye una afirmación, dos razones, por lo menos una evidencia y una respuesta a alguien que podría no estar de acuerdo.

Sentence starters: I believe ___. The first reason is ___. For example, ___. The second reason is ___. Some people might say ___, but ___.

Challenge: label each sentence: claim, reason, evidence or counterclaim.

Reto: señala cada oración: afirmación, razón, evidencia o contraargumento.

Check your own work

Read your answers again. Press each sentence that is true about your work.

Vuelve a leer tus respuestas. Pulsa cada oración que sea verdadera sobre tu trabajo.

Part 1. Daniel's claim is that video games, played for a limited time, can be good for kids. The claim “First, some games can sharpen your brain” is supported, because Daniel gives evidence from scientists who found that players got better at noticing details. The claim “Video games make kids smarter than kids who read books” is not supported, because the essay never compares gamers with readers and gives no study, number or example for it.

Challenge. In paragraph 4, Daniel admits that too much gaming can hurt sleep and health. He answers by suggesting a limit of one hour a day and never right before bed. This makes his argument stronger, because it shows he is fair and has thought about the other side.

Part 2. “I believe our school should have a reading corner in every classroom. The first reason is that students read more when books are close. For example, in our class, students checked out twice as many books after Ms. Rivera added a small shelf. The second reason is that a reading corner gives students a quiet place to calm down. Some people might say that there is no money for new books, but families and the public library could donate books they no longer use.”

Challenge. Claim: I believe… Reason: The first reason… Evidence: For example… Reason: The second reason… Counterclaim and answer: Some people might say…, but…

Your words can be different. What matters is that you can tell supported claims from unsupported ones and that your own argument has a claim, reasons and evidence.

Aligned to the Illinois sixth grade English Language Arts standards RI.6.6 (determine an author's point of view or purpose in a text and explain how it is conveyed in the text), RI.6.8 (trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text, distinguishing claims that are supported by reasons and evidence from claims that are not) and RI.6.9 (compare and contrast one author's presentation of events with that of another, e.g., a memoir written by and a biography on the same person). The three essays are written by sixth graders and include reasons, evidence, a counterclaim and at least one unsupported claim on purpose; the two pairs compare a news article with a memoir and a school newsletter with a personal blog about the same event. At the Easy level there are fewer options and the sentence being studied is highlighted; at the Challenge level the student must prove the answer: point to the sentence that shows the author's point of view, find a reason after the claim, classify counterclaims and unsupported claims, and explain why a claim is or is not supported. Every wrong answer gets a hint that explains the mistake (for example, mistaking a reason for the claim, or treating “everyone knows” as evidence). The final task asks the student to judge an argument and to write a short argument with a claim, reasons, evidence and an answer to the other side. The optional “Ayuda en español” button translates only the instruction or the question, never the texts or the answers, so the reading stays in English (WIDA-friendly support for English learners).
