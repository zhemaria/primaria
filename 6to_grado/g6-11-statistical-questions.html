Part 1. Elías's question is statistical, because different students sleep different numbers of hours, so we expect many answers that vary. Salma's question has only one answer, the hours she slept last night. To collect the data, I would ask each sixth grader (or a large group of them) how many hours they usually sleep on a school night, and write every answer down.

Part 2. In order: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 9, 10, 10, 11. The dot plot has one dot at 6, one at 7, two at 8, four at 9, two at 10 and one at 11. The median (the middle value, the 6th of 11) is 9 hours. The range is 11 − 6 = 5 hours. The data cluster around 9, with fewer values far from it, so the shape is roughly symmetric, like a hill with its peak at 9.

Challenge. “How many hours do I sleep on school nights this month?” is statistical, because the answer changes from night to night. If one more student says 3 hours, the range becomes 11 − 3 = 8, which is much bigger; the median stays at 9 (it is the middle of 12 values: the 6th and 7th are 9 and 9). The range changes more because it uses only the two extreme values, and the median depends on the middle.

Aligned to the Illinois sixth grade mathematics standards 6.SP.1 (recognize a statistical question as one that anticipates variability in the data related to the question and accounts for it in the answers; for example, “How old am I?” is not a statistical question, but “How old are the students in my school?” is), 6.SP.2 (understand that a set of data collected to answer a statistical question has a distribution which can be described by its center, spread and overall shape) and 6.SP.3 (recognize that a measure of center for a numerical data set summarizes all of its values with a single number, while a measure of variation describes how its values vary with a single number). At the Easy level the dot plots are small, the data are already in order and the questions use everyday words (most common, highest, lowest); at the Challenge level the child finds the median of an even number of values, compares the spread of two sets, describes shape with words such as symmetric, skewed, cluster, gap and outlier, decides which measure answers a question, and in “Who is right?” must also choose the reason. Typical mistakes get their own hints: thinking a question about one person is statistical, taking the middle of the list before ordering it, giving the largest value instead of the range, and confusing the most common value with the median. The optional “Ayuda en español” button translates only the instruction or the question; the game itself stays in English. Each game has its own direct link: #pregunta, #puntos, #centro, #dispersion, #forma and #quien, and the final task (build and explain an answer without options) is #tarea.

Alineado con los estándares de matemáticas de Illinois para sexto grado 6.SP.1 (reconocer una pregunta estadística como la que anticipa variabilidad en los datos; por ejemplo, «¿Cuántos años tengo?» no es estadística, pero «¿Cuántos años tienen los estudiantes de mi escuela?» sí lo es), 6.SP.2 (un conjunto de datos tiene una distribución que se describe por su centro, su dispersión y su forma) y 6.SP.3 (una medida de centro resume todos los valores en un solo número, mientras que una medida de variación describe con un solo número cómo varían). En el nivel Fácil los diagramas de puntos son pequeños y los datos ya están ordenados; en el Reto se halla la mediana de un número par de datos, se compara la dispersión de dos conjuntos, se describe la forma con palabras como simétrica, sesgada, agrupación, hueco y valor atípico, y se decide qué medida responde una pregunta. Los errores típicos tienen su propia pista: pensar que una pregunta sobre una sola persona es estadística, tomar el valor del medio sin ordenar la lista, dar el valor mayor en lugar del rango y confundir el valor más frecuente con la mediana. Desde quinto grado los juegos son en inglés; el botón «Ayuda en español» traduce solo la consigna o la pregunta.
