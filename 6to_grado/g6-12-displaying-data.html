Part 1 · Explain

Israel scored these points in 9 basketball games: 8, 10, 7, 9, 11, 8, 30, 9, 10. Find the mean and the median. Sophia says the mean is a good way to describe a typical game. Is she right? Explain which measure describes a typical game better, and why.

Israel anotó estos puntos en 9 partidos de baloncesto: 8, 10, 7, 9, 11, 8, 30, 9, 10. Halla la media y la mediana. Sophia dice que la media es una buena manera de describir un partido típico. ¿Tiene razón? Explica qué medida describe mejor un partido típico y por qué.

Sentence starters: The sum is ___, so the mean is ___ ÷ 9 = ___. In order, the data are ___, so the median is ___. The value ___ is an outlier, so the ___ describes a typical game better because ___.

Part 2 · Apply the idea to a new problem

Five friends have $4, $6, $7, $8 and $10. They put all their money together and share it equally. How much does each one get? Explain why that amount is the mean. Then tell how many friends were in the data, what was measured, and in what unit.

Cinco amigos tienen $4, $6, $7, $8 y $10. Juntan todo su dinero y lo reparten en partes iguales. ¿Cuánto le toca a cada uno? Explica por qué esa cantidad es la media. Después di cuántos amigos había en los datos, qué se midió y en qué unidad.

Sentence starters: Together they have $___. Shared by 5, each gets $___. This is the mean because ___. There were ___ observations; the attribute measured was ___, in ___.

Challenge: a sixth friend joins with $1. How do the mean and the median change? Which one changes more?

Reto: se une un sexto amigo con $1. ¿Cómo cambian la media y la mediana? ¿Cuál cambia más?

Part 1. The sum is 8 + 10 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 8 + 30 + 9 + 10 = 102, so the mean is 102 ÷ 9 ≈ 11.3 points. In order: 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10, 10, 11, 30, so the median is 9 (the 5th of 9 values). Sophia is not right: the 30 is an outlier and pulls the mean up, so the mean is higher than 8 of the 9 games. The median, 9 points, describes a typical game better, because it doesn't depend on how big the outlier is.

Part 2. Together they have 4 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 10 = $35. Shared by 5, each gets 35 ÷ 5 = $7. That is the mean: the amount each one would have if the total were shared equally. There were 5 observations; the attribute measured was the money each friend had, in dollars.

Challenge. Five-number summary: minimum 7, lower quartile 8, median 9, upper quartile 10.5, maximum 30. IQR = 10.5 − 8 = 2.5. The box goes from 8 to 10.5 with a line at 9; the left whisker reaches 7 and the right whisker stretches all the way to 30. With a sixth friend with $1: the mean becomes 36 ÷ 6 = $6 (down $1), and the median becomes (6 + 7) ÷ 2 = $6.50 (down $0.50). The mean changes more.

Aligned to the Illinois sixth grade mathematics standards 6.SP.4 (display numerical data in plots on a number line, including dot plots, histograms and box plots) and 6.SP.5 (summarize numerical data sets in relation to their context: a) report the number of observations; b) describe the attribute under investigation, how it was measured and its units; c) give quantitative measures of center (median and/or mean) and variability (interquartile range and/or mean absolute deviation), and describe any overall pattern and any striking deviations; d) relate the choice of measures of center and variability to the shape of the distribution and the context). At the Easy level the mean is found by leveling bars, the histograms are labeled and the data for the five-number summary are already in order; at the Challenge level the child finds the mean of unordered data, reads intervals in histograms, builds and reads box plots, finds the interquartile range and the mean absolute deviation, chooses the mean or the median for a context with an outlier, and in “Who is right?” must also choose the reason. Typical mistakes get their own hints: dividing by the wrong count, reading a histogram bar as one value, confusing the IQR with the range, and choosing the mean when an outlier pulls it away from the typical values.
