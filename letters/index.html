Letters

Three activities for the hand and the eye in pre-K. Use them in this order: first the hand learns to move, then it draws letters, and last it learns to recognize them.

  1. 1

    Tracing paths

    Lines and shapes before any letter. The child learns to go down, to cross from left to right, and to close a shape.

    Eight exercises: down, across, slants both ways, waves, zigzag, circle and closed shapes.

    Open module one
  2. 2

    Capital letters step by step

    All 26 letters with their starting point, stroke order and direction. Grouped by what the hand has to do, not alphabetically.

    Includes building the child's own name so they can trace it letter by letter.

    Open module two
  3. 3

    Knowing the letters

    Recognizing the letter with the eye and the ear instead of the hand.

    Three activities: matching capital with lowercase, connecting the dots in alphabetical order, and picking the word that starts with each letter.

    Open module three

For the grown-up

How to use the series

The modules are ordered by what depends on what, not by difficulty within a single session. A child may spend weeks on the first one before touching the second, and that is what you should expect. Sessions of five to eight minutes are enough.

All three modules have an easy mode, an animated demonstration, and the option to mark an exercise as done on paper, so no child gets stuck for lack of motor control.

What each one practices

  • The first one, stroke control and direction: top to bottom and left to right.
  • The second one, letter formation and the idea that a letter is always written the same way, starting from the same place.
  • The third one, the match between capital and lowercase, alphabetical order, and beginning sounds.

Standards

The series supports the Illinois Early Learning and Development Standards for language and literacy at this age: early writing, print awareness and letter recognition. Four and five year olds are not asked for handwriting, they are asked to recognize letters and know where each one starts.

In the classroom

Keep the tablet flat on the table, never held in the hand. Alternate the screen with paper and crayon: a screen offers no resistance, and resistance is part of what strengthens the hand.
