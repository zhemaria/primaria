Galileo and the rolling balls

Galileo Galilei was an Italian scientist who wanted to know how things fall. The problem was that a falling stone is too fast to measure with the clocks of the 1600s. So he had a clever idea: slow the fall down. He let bronze balls roll down a long, smooth groove cut in a wooden board, tilted like a ramp.

A clock made of water. To measure the time, he let water run out of a big container through a thin pipe while the ball rolled, collected it in a cup and weighed it. More water meant more time. He repeated each run many times. Some historians think he also used musical beats, because he was a good lute player.

The rule. Galileo found that in equal times the ball covered distances in the pattern 1, 3, 5, 7… The totals were 1, 4, 9, 16: the distance is proportional to the time squared. Each time has exactly one distance, so distance is a function of time. But it is not a straight line: it is a nonlinear function, because the ball keeps going faster.

Nova: 1, 4, 9, 16… Those are perfect squares!

Lumi: Yes. If the ball went at a steady speed, the distances would grow by the same amount each time, like 2, 4, 6, 8. That would be linear.

Nova: So a table can tell me if a function is linear: I look at how much it changes each time.

Lumi: Exactly. That is the rate of change. Let’s find it in tables, equations and graphs.

Galileo published these results in 1638 in his book Two New Sciences, when he was old and under house arrest; the book had to be printed in Leiden, in the Netherlands. In 1914, two professors of Northwestern University, in Evanston, Illinois, Henry Crew and Alfonso de Salvio, translated it into English, and their translation is still read today.

Galileo Galilei fue un científico italiano que quería saber cómo caen las cosas. El problema era que una piedra que cae es demasiado rápida para medirla con los relojes del siglo XVII. Así que tuvo una idea ingeniosa: hacer más lenta la caída. Dejó rodar bolas de bronce por una ranura larga y lisa tallada en una tabla de madera inclinada como una rampa. Para medir el tiempo, dejaba salir agua de un recipiente grande por un tubo delgado mientras la bola rodaba, la recogía en una taza y la pesaba: más agua significaba más tiempo. Algunos historiadores creen que también usó compases musicales, porque tocaba bien el laúd. Galileo descubrió que, en tiempos iguales, la bola recorría distancias que seguían el patrón 1, 3, 5, 7…, y que los totales eran 1, 4, 9, 16: la distancia es proporcional al cuadrado del tiempo. Cada tiempo tiene exactamente una distancia, así que la distancia es una función del tiempo, pero no es lineal, porque la bola va cada vez más rápido. Publicó estos resultados en 1638 en su libro Dos nuevas ciencias, ya anciano y bajo arresto domiciliario; el libro tuvo que imprimirse en Leiden, en los Países Bajos. En 1914, dos profesores de la Universidad Northwestern, en Evanston (Illinois), Henry Crew y Alfonso de Salvio, lo tradujeron al inglés.

Galileo’s notes show that he was already working on these experiments in the early 1600s, more than thirty years before the book was published. You can try the experiment yourself with a long board, a marble, tape to mark the distances and a stopwatch.

Part 1. Daniel is not right. Going up is not enough to be linear. From one time to the next, the distance grows by 3, then 5, then 7: the change is not constant. In a linear function the rate of change is always the same, and the graph is a straight line. Galileo’s function is a function (each time has one distance), but it is nonlinear: distance = time², and its graph is a curve.

Challenge. At time 6 the distance is 6² = 36, and at time 10 it is 10² = 100. During the 6th interval alone the ball goes 36 − 25 = 11, the next odd number after 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.

Part 2. Athena is not right: she swapped the two numbers. The $25 is paid every month, so it is the rate of change (the slope). The $40 is paid once, at the start, so it is the initial value (the y-intercept). The function is y = 25x + 40, where x is the number of months and y is the total cost.

Challenge. 25x + 40 = 30x + 10 gives 30 = 5x, so x = 6 months: both plans cost $190. After 10 months, plan A costs 25(10) + 40 = $290 and plan B costs 30(10) + 10 = $310, so plan B costs more.
