Hubble’s galaxies and Fisher’s tea

A boy from Wheaton. Edwin Hubble grew up in Wheaton, Illinois, west of Chicago. He studied at the University of Chicago, where he received his degree in 1910, and he was chosen as a Rhodes Scholar from Illinois to study in England. Later he became an astronomer at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California.

A graph of dots that changed the universe. In 1929 Hubble published a short article with a graph. Each dot was a galaxy: across, its distance from us; up, the speed at which it moves away. He had distances for only 24 galaxies, but the dots showed a clear pattern: the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away. The dots did not fall exactly on a line, but a straight line fit them well. That pattern was a key clue that the universe is expanding. Today the Hubble Space Telescope carries his name.

A cup of tea in England. Around the same years, in the 1920s, the statistician Ronald Fisher worked at Rothamsted, a farm research station in England, where crops gave “noisy” data: very similar fields gave different harvests. One day he offered a cup of tea to a scientist who studied algae, Muriel Bristol. She refused it: she said she could taste whether the milk had been poured before or after the tea. Fisher designed a fair test: 8 cups, 4 of each kind, in random order. If she were only guessing, the chance of getting all 8 right is just 1 in 70. The story says she got them right; historians still discuss exactly how the test went. Fisher told the story in his book The Design of Experiments (1935), and his ideas about testing with chance are still used today, from medicine to data science.

Nova: Hubble’s dots weren’t on a perfect line, but he still found the pattern.

Lumi: That is the secret of data: real data is noisy. We look for the trend, and we ask if it could be just chance.

Nova: Like the tea: one lucky guess is easy, but 8 out of 8 is hard to explain by luck.

Lumi: Exactly. Let’s find patterns in scatter plots and in tables.

Edwin Hubble creció en Wheaton, Illinois, al oeste de Chicago. Estudió en la Universidad de Chicago, donde se graduó en 1910, y fue elegido becario Rhodes por Illinois para estudiar en Inglaterra. Después fue astrónomo en el observatorio de Monte Wilson, en California. En 1929 publicó un artículo breve con una gráfica: cada punto era una galaxia; en horizontal, su distancia; en vertical, la velocidad con que se aleja. Solo tenía distancias de 24 galaxias, pero los puntos mostraban un patrón claro: cuanto más lejos está una galaxia, más rápido se aleja. Los puntos no caían exactamente en una recta, pero una recta los ajustaba bien. Ese patrón fue una pista clave de que el universo se expande. En los mismos años, en la década de 1920, el estadístico Ronald Fisher trabajaba en Rothamsted, una estación de investigación agrícola de Inglaterra. Un día le ofreció una taza de té a una científica que estudiaba algas, Muriel Bristol, que la rechazó: decía que notaba si la leche se había servido antes o después del té. Fisher diseñó una prueba justa: 8 tazas, 4 de cada tipo, en orden al azar. Si solo adivinara, la probabilidad de acertar las 8 es de 1 entre 70. La historia cuenta que acertó; los historiadores todavía discuten cómo fue exactamente la prueba. Fisher la contó en su libro El diseño de experimentos (1935).

Hubble’s 1929 article was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. The scatter plots in these games use numbers like his, simplified for the classroom, not his exact measurements.

Part 1 · Explain

A scatter plot shows 10 dots that go up to the right. Athena draws a line of fit through the first dot and the last dot, but 8 of the other dots are above her line. Is it a good line of fit? Explain what a good line of fit looks like.

Un diagrama de dispersión muestra 10 puntos que suben hacia la derecha. Athena traza una recta de ajuste por el primer punto y el último, pero 8 de los demás puntos quedan por encima de su recta. ¿Es una buena recta de ajuste? Explica cómo es una buena recta de ajuste.

Sentence starters: A good line of fit follows the ___ of the dots. About the same number of dots should be ___ and ___ the line, and they should be ___ to it. Athena’s line ___ because ___.

Useful words: scatter plot, trend, association, line of fit, above, below, close.

Challenge: for data like Hubble’s, a line of fit is v = 500d, where d is the distance (in millions of parsecs) and v is the speed (in km/s). Predict the speed of a galaxy at a distance of 1.6. What does the 500 mean?

Reto: para datos como los de Hubble, una recta de ajuste es v = 500d, donde d es la distancia (en millones de pársecs) y v la velocidad (en km/s). Predice la velocidad de una galaxia a una distancia de 1.6. ¿Qué significa el 500?

Part 1. It is not a good line of fit. A line of fit does not have to touch any dot; it should follow the trend of all the dots, with about the same number of dots above and below it, and as close to the dots as possible. Athena’s line has 8 dots above it and none below, so it is too low. A better line would be a little higher, through the middle of the cloud of dots.

Challenge. v = 500 × 1.6 = 800 km/s. The 500 is the slope: for each additional million parsecs of distance, the speed goes up by about 500 km/s. Because the line goes through the origin, a galaxy twice as far moves away about twice as fast.

Part 2. The table: instrument and sport 12, instrument and no sport 8 (total 20); no instrument and sport 18, neither 12 (total 30). Of the 20 students who play an instrument, 12 play a sport: 12/20 = 60%. Of the 30 who don’t, 18 play a sport: 18/30 = 60%. The percents are the same, so playing an instrument and playing a sport are not associated in this group. Selena is right that most instrument players play a sport, but so do most of the others.

Challenge. The table would be: really milk first → 3 said milk first, 1 said tea first; really tea first → 1 said milk first, 3 said tea first. 17 out of 70 is about 24%, about 1 chance in 4, so guessing could easily give that result. It would not convince me. Getting all 8 right (1 in 70, about 1.4%) is much harder to explain by luck.
