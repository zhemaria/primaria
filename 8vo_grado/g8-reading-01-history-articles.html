The article: A Cup of Tea That Changed Science

1One afternoon in the 1920s, at a farm research station in England called Rothamsted, a statistician named Ronald Fisher poured a cup of tea. He offered it to Muriel Bristol, a scientist who studied algae. She refused it, saying that she preferred her tea when the milk was poured into the cup first.

2Fisher did not believe that anyone could taste the difference. But instead of arguing, the scientists decided to test her claim. According to the story, William Roach, who later married her, suggested how to do it.

3Fisher planned the test with great care. There would be eight cups: four with the milk poured first and four with the tea poured first, served in random order. Bristol would taste each one and say which was which.

4The design mattered because guessing can sometimes look like skill. If she were only guessing, the chance of getting all eight cups right would be just 1 in 70. The story says she got them right, although historians still discuss exactly how the test went.

5Fisher wrote about this test in his book The Design of Experiments, published in 1935. His idea was simple but powerful: before believing a claim, ask how likely the result would be by chance alone. Today, scientists use that idea to test new medicines, and data scientists use it to find real patterns in noisy data.

Part 1. Central idea: a small disagreement about tea led Ronald Fisher to design a careful test, and his idea of checking results against chance is still used in science. Paragraph 3 develops it by describing the careful design (eight cups, four of each kind, in random order), and paragraph 4 explains why the design mattered: guessing all eight right would happen only 1 time in 70. Summary: In the 1920s, Muriel Bristol said she could taste whether the milk had been poured into her tea first. Ronald Fisher designed a test with eight cups in random order, so that a lucky guess would be very unlikely. He described the test in 1935, and today scientists use his idea to test medicines and to find patterns in data.

Challenge. The sentence shows that the author is honest about what we know and what we don’t. Instead of presenting a legend as a certain fact, the author separates the story from the history, and that makes the reader trust the rest of the article more.

Part 2. It started with a cup of tea: “She refused it, saying that she preferred her tea when the milk was poured into the cup first.” This small disagreement led Fisher to design a fair test instead of arguing. The article says, “If she were only guessing, the chance of getting all eight cups right would be just 1 in 70,” which shows the big idea: a result is convincing only if chance alone would rarely produce it. Finally, “Today, scientists use that idea to test new medicines, and data scientists use it to find real patterns in noisy data,” so the tea test connects to science today.

Challenge. Like Fisher, Hubble and Galileo did not just trust their opinions: they collected careful measurements and looked for a pattern. Hubble had noisy data, and Fisher asked whether a result could be chance; both looked for evidence strong enough to believe.
