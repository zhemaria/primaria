The tablet of the square root of 2

About 3,700 years ago, in the city-schools of Babylon, in what is now Iraq, students learned mathematics by writing on wet clay with a reed. One of their tablets is round and fits in the palm of your hand. It is known as YBC 7289, because it belongs to the Yale Babylonian Collection, at Yale University in Connecticut.

What is on the tablet. A square with both diagonals. One side says 30. Along a diagonal there are two numbers written in base 60, the same base we still use for minutes and seconds. The first one, 1;24,51,10, is the square root of 2, the number that multiplied by itself gives 2. In our decimals it is about 1.41421296, and the true value is 1.41421356…: the student got it right to six decimal places. The second number, 42;25,35, is the diagonal of a square of side 30, that is, 30 × √2.

Why √2 is special. The Babylonians used √2 very well, but more than a thousand years later Greek mathematicians proved something surprising: no fraction is exactly equal to √2. Its decimals go on forever and never repeat. Numbers like this are called irrational. Legend says the discovery came from a follower of Pythagoras named Hippasus, and that it upset the other members of the school, because they believed every number could be written as a fraction.

Nova: So the Babylonian answer is wrong?

Lumi: Not wrong: it is an excellent approximation. Nobody can write all of √2, because its decimals never end. We can only get closer and closer.

Nova: And 30 × √2 is the diagonal… That is the Pythagorean theorem!

Lumi: Exactly: 30² + 30² = 1,800, and √1,800 = 30 × √2. The Babylonians knew it more than a thousand years before Pythagoras.

In these games you will find square roots between whole numbers, like the Babylonians, and you will meet numbers that are too big or too small to write easily. For those, we use powers of 10.

Hace unos 3700 años, en las escuelas de Babilonia, en lo que hoy es Irak, los estudiantes aprendían matemáticas escribiendo con una caña sobre barro húmedo. Una de sus tablillas es redonda y cabe en la palma de la mano; se conoce como YBC 7289 porque pertenece a la Colección Babilónica de Yale, en la Universidad de Yale (Connecticut). Tiene un cuadrado con sus dos diagonales; un lado dice 30. Sobre una diagonal hay dos números escritos en base 60, la misma que seguimos usando para los minutos y los segundos. El primero, 1;24,51,10, es la raíz cuadrada de 2, el número que multiplicado por sí mismo da 2: en nuestros decimales es 1.41421296, y el valor verdadero es 1.41421356…, así que el estudiante acertó hasta el sexto decimal. El segundo, 42;25,35, es la diagonal de un cuadrado de lado 30, es decir, 30 × √2. Más de mil años después, los matemáticos griegos demostraron que ninguna fracción es exactamente igual a √2: sus decimales siguen para siempre y nunca se repiten. A estos números se les llama irracionales. Cuenta la leyenda que el descubrimiento vino de un seguidor de Pitágoras llamado Hipaso, y que molestó a los demás miembros de la escuela, que creían que todo número podía escribirse como fracción.

The tablet YBC 7289 is about 8 centimeters across and was made between about 1800 and 1600 BCE. The sign “;” separates the whole part from the sixtieths, the way a decimal point separates the whole part from the tenths. Pictures of the tablet can be seen on the website of the Yale Peabody Museum.

Part 1. Athena is not right. 1.41421296 × 1.41421296 ≈ 1.9999983, which is very close to 2 but not equal to 2. So the Babylonian value is an excellent approximation, not the exact value. √2 is irrational: no fraction is exactly equal to it, and its decimals go on forever without repeating. Any decimal we write down, like 1.41421296, is rational, so it can only be an approximation of √2.

Challenge. 1.4² = 1.96 and 1.5² = 2.25, so √2 is between 1.4 and 1.5. 1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164, so √2 is between 1.41 and 1.42.

Part 2. Daniel is not right. 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 and 2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. Multiplied together there are 3 + 4 = 7 twos, so 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128, not 2¹² = 4,096. When we multiply powers with the same base, we add the exponents. Another example: 5² × 5³ = 5⁵.

Challenge. Selena is not right. 9 × 10⁵ = 900,000 and 4.5 × 10⁶ = 4,500,000. In scientific notation, we compare the powers of 10 first: 10⁶ is ten times 10⁵. 4,500,000 ÷ 900,000 = 5, so 4.5 × 10⁶ is 5 times bigger.
