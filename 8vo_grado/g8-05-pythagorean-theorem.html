Garfield’s trapezoid

The idea is very old. About 3,800 years ago, in Babylon, a scribe wrote on a clay tablet a list of numbers that fit right triangles. That tablet, called Plimpton 322, is kept today at Columbia University in New York. About 2,500 years ago, the Greek thinker Pythagoras and his followers made the idea famous, and it took his name: in a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the legs, a² + b² = c².

A congressman who loved math. James A. Garfield grew up poor on a farm in Ohio. He studied hard, became a teacher and the president of a small college, then a general in the Civil War, and later a member of Congress. He liked to relax by doing mathematics. In 1876 he found his own proof of the Pythagorean theorem. In his diary, on March 7, 1876, he wrote that he showed it to mathematics professors at Dartmouth College. It was printed on April 1, 1876, in the New-England Journal of Education. The editors joked that it was something the members of both parties in Congress could agree on.

The trapezoid. Garfield put two copies of a right triangle, with legs a and b and hypotenuse c, next to each other, and joined them with a third triangle whose two legs are both c. Together they make a trapezoid. Then he found its area in two ways: with the formula for a trapezoid, and by adding the areas of the three triangles. Both ways must give the same area, and when you make them equal, you get a² + b² = c².

Nova: So he didn’t just check a few triangles. He proved it works for every right triangle!

Lumi: Exactly. That is the difference between an example and a proof.

Nova: And did he become president?

Lumi: Yes. In 1880 he was elected the 20th president of the United States. He is the only president who published an original proof in mathematics.

Today people know hundreds of different proofs of this theorem; a book from 1940 collected 370 of them, and Garfield’s is in it. Now it is your turn to work like Garfield.

La idea es muy antigua. Hace unos 3800 años, en Babilonia, un escriba anotó en una tablilla de barro una lista de números que encajan en triángulos rectángulos; esa tablilla, llamada Plimpton 322, se guarda hoy en la Universidad de Columbia, en Nueva York. Hace unos 2500 años, el pensador griego Pitágoras y sus seguidores hicieron famosa la idea, que lleva su nombre: en un triángulo rectángulo, el cuadrado de la hipotenusa es igual a la suma de los cuadrados de los catetos, a² + b² = c². James A. Garfield creció pobre en una granja de Ohio; estudió mucho, fue maestro y director de un pequeño colegio, general en la Guerra Civil y después congresista. Se relajaba haciendo matemáticas. En 1876 encontró su propia demostración del teorema: el 7 de marzo de 1876 anotó en su diario que se la mostró a profesores de matemáticas del Dartmouth College, y se publicó el 1 de abril de 1876 en el New-England Journal of Education. Los editores bromearon diciendo que era algo en lo que los congresistas de los dos partidos podían estar de acuerdo. Garfield juntó dos copias de un triángulo rectángulo (catetos a y b, hipotenusa c) con un tercer triángulo de catetos c y c, y formó un trapecio. Calculó su área de dos maneras, con la fórmula del trapecio y sumando los tres triángulos; al igualarlas obtuvo a² + b² = c². En 1880 fue elegido el 20.º presidente de los Estados Unidos, y es el único presidente que publicó una demostración matemática original.

The book of 1940 is The Pythagorean Proposition, by Elisha S. Loomis. Garfield was president for only a few months: he was shot in July 1881 and died that September. This story is also told in the book Geometría Plana: Lo que debes saber, by José M. Fernández.

Part 1. Elías is not right. The Pythagorean theorem does not add the sides; it adds the squares of the sides. In any triangle, two sides together are always longer than the third side, so 8 + 15 will never equal 17. Let’s test the squares: 8² + 15² = 64 + 225 = 289, and 17² = 289. They are equal, so by the converse of the Pythagorean theorem the triangle is a right triangle. The right angle is opposite the longest side, 17 cm.

Challenge. The trapezoid has parallel sides 8 and 15 and height 8 + 15 = 23. Area of the trapezoid: ½ × (8 + 15) × 23 = ½ × 529 = 264.5. Area of the three triangles: ½ × 8 × 15 + ½ × 8 × 15 + ½ × 17 × 17 = 60 + 60 + 144.5 = 264.5. The two areas are equal.

Part 2. Salma is not right. The ladder is the hypotenuse, because it is across from the right angle between the wall and the floor. 5² + h² = 13², so h² = 169 − 25 = 144, and h = 12. The ladder reaches 12 feet up the wall. Subtracting the lengths does not work; we subtract the squares.

Challenge. Selena is not right: 14 is the walk along the grid lines. The horizontal change is 4 − (−2) = 6 and the vertical change is 9 − 1 = 8. The straight distance is the hypotenuse: √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10.

Your words can be different. What matters is that your answer shows the math and explains how it proves your answer.
