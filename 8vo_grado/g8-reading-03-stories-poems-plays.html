Old myths, new stories

Thales measures a pyramid without climbing it, while the priests laugh. A Roman searches for the tomb of Archimedes under the thorns. A scientist says she can taste whether the milk was poured first, and nobody knows how her test ended. And in Chicago, Leo flies a drone named ICARUS over the lake, while Maya accepts a deal that doubles every day, just like a wish of King Midas. The story: Nice Try, Sami (a modern story)

Sami loved to make his friends panic. Twice that fall, he posted in the class group chat, “Mr. Ortiz says the field trip is canceled!” and laughed when twenty phones buzzed at once.

The third time, his friends only answered with laughing faces.

On the morning of the real field trip to the Field Museum, Sami saw a note taped to the school door: the bus would leave at 7:30, not at 8:30. He posted the news in the chat in capital letters.

“Nice try, Sami,” Ella wrote. After that, nobody answered.

At 7:30, Sami climbed onto the bus with only four classmates. The rest of the class arrived at 8:30 to an empty parking lot.

That afternoon, Sami didn't post anything. He walked to Ella's locker and said, “I'm sorry. I made my own words worth nothing.”

The old fable (from Aesop): A shepherd boy who was bored on the hillside shouted “Wolf! Wolf!” to make the villagers run up the hill, and he laughed at them. He played the trick twice. When a real wolf came, he shouted again, but this time nobody came, because nobody believed him. The fable ends with a lesson: nobody believes a liar, even when he tells the truth.

Part 1 · An old fable, a new story

How does the modern story draw on the fable of the boy who cried wolf? Name the pattern of events and the type of character that both texts share. Then explain two ways the author renders the fable new. Quote the story at least twice, with quotation marks.

¿Cómo se basa el cuento moderno en la fábula del pastorcito mentiroso? Nombra la secuencia de sucesos y el tipo de personaje que comparten los dos textos. Después explica dos maneras en que el autor renueva la fábula. Cita el cuento por lo menos dos veces, entre comillas.

Sentence starters: Both texts follow the same pattern: first ___, then ___, finally ___. Sami is like the shepherd boy because ___: “___.” The author renders the fable new by ___ and by ___: “___.”

Useful words: pattern of events, character type, fable, modern, allusion, renders new, theme, turning point, evidence.

Challenge: the fable states its lesson in the last line, but the story doesn't. How does the story show its theme instead?

Reto: la fábula dice su lección en la última línea, pero el cuento no. ¿Cómo muestra el cuento su tema?

Part 2 · Dramatic irony and dialogue

When Ella writes “Nice try, Sami,” the reader knows something that Ella doesn't. Explain this dramatic irony and the feeling it creates. Then choose one line of dialogue from the story and explain how it moves the action, reveals Sami's character or leads to a decision.

Cuando Ella escribe «Nice try, Sami», el lector sabe algo que Ella no sabe. Explica esta ironía dramática y lo que te hace sentir. Después elige una línea de diálogo del cuento y explica cómo mueve la acción, revela cómo es Sami o lleva a una decisión.

Sentence starters: The reader knows that ___, but Ella thinks ___. This creates ___. The line “___” ___ because ___.

Challenge: write the first four or five sentences of your own modern story that draws on a myth or a fable you know. Include one line of dialogue.

Reto: escribe las primeras cuatro o cinco oraciones de tu propio cuento moderno basado en un mito o una fábula que conozcas. Incluye una línea de diálogo.

Part 1. Both texts follow the same pattern: a boy plays a trick that makes people panic, he repeats it, and when the danger is real, nobody believes him. Sami is the same type of character as the shepherd boy, a prankster who enjoys other people’s fear: he “laughed when twenty phones buzzed at once.” The author renders the fable new in two ways. First, the wolf becomes a field trip and the hillside becomes a group chat: “he posted in the class group chat.” Second, the ending is different: nobody is hurt, and Sami takes responsibility instead of only being punished: “I’m sorry. I made my own words worth nothing.”

Challenge. The story never states its lesson. It shows it through what happens: only four classmates get on the bus, and Sami understands his mistake and apologizes. The reader figures out the theme, that lies make people stop trusting even your true words, from his actions and his last line.

Part 2. When Ella writes “Nice try, Sami,” the reader knows that this time the message is true, because we saw the note on the school door. Ella thinks it is another prank. This dramatic irony creates suspense and a sinking feeling, because we can see the class is going to miss the bus. The line “I made my own words worth nothing” reveals Sami’s character at the end: he finally understands what his pranks cost, and it shows that he has changed.

Challenge. “Nobody in our building believed Grace could beat Tomás in the stairs race. Tomás was the fastest boy on the South Side, and he never stopped telling everyone. ‘I could win with my eyes closed,’ he said, and he stopped on the fourth floor to post a video. Grace just kept climbing, one step at a time.” (This beginning draws on the fable of the tortoise and the hare.)

Aligned to the Illinois eighth grade English Language Arts standards RL.8.1 (cite the textual evidence that most strongly supports an analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text), RL.8.2 (determine a theme or central idea and analyze its development, including its relationship to the characters, setting and plot; provide an objective summary), RL.8.3 (analyze how particular lines of dialogue or incidents in a story or drama propel the action, reveal aspects of a character or provoke a decision), RL.8.4 (determine the meaning of words and phrases, including figurative and connotative meanings; analyze the impact of specific word choices on meaning and tone, including analogies or allusions to other texts), RL.8.5 (compare and contrast the structure of two or more texts and analyze how the differing structure of each text contributes to its meaning and style), RL.8.6 (analyze how differences in the points of view of the characters and the audience or reader, created through the use of dramatic irony, create such effects as suspense or humor) and RL.8.9 (analyze how a modern work of fiction draws on themes, patterns of events or character types from myths, traditional stories or religious works, including describing how the material is rendered new). The five texts connect with the eighth grade math stories: historical fiction about Thales measuring the pyramid with shadows, a poem based on Cicero's true account of finding the tomb of Archimedes (75 BCE), a play inspired by the lady tasting tea at Rothamsted, which leaves her real answers unknown, as history does, and two modern Chicago stories that draw on the myths of Icarus and King Midas, with the 3-4-5 triangle and powers of two. The seventh game pairs each myth or fable (Ovid, Aesop) with a modern text. The invented people and words are labeled as fiction. At the Easy level Lumi reads the text aloud while the sentence is highlighted, there are fewer options and the evidence is marked after a mistake; at the Challenge level the texts are longer and the student must prove each answer by choosing the sentence, line or stanza that supports it. Every wrong answer gets a hint, never the answer. The final task compares a modern story with the fable of the boy who cried wolf and asks the student to explain its dramatic irony. The optional “Ayuda en español” button translates only the instruction or the question, never the text or the answers, so the reading stays in English. Each game has its own direct link: #escucha, #citar, #tema, #comparar, #estructura, #narrador, #mito and #tarea.
