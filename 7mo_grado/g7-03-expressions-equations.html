Ahmes and the “aha” problems

Do you remember Ahmes, the scribe of the bread? His papyrus has more surprises. Some of its problems are about an unknown amount that the Egyptians called aha, a word that means “quantity” or “heap.” Problem 24 says: A quantity and its seventh part, added together, make 19. What is the quantity?

Today we would write x + x/7 = 19. Ahmes did not use letters. He used a method called false position: he tried a convenient number on purpose. He chose 7, because its seventh part is easy: 7 + 1 = 8. But the answer had to be 19, not 8. So he asked: what do I multiply 8 by to get 19? 19 ÷ 8 = 2 3/8. Then he multiplied his guess by the same number: 7 × 2 3/8 = 16 5/8. The quantity is 16 5/8, and the scribe checks it: 16 5/8 + 2 3/8 = 19.

Nova: He guessed on purpose, and the wrong guess helped him find the right answer?

Lumi: Yes! It works because the relationship is proportional: if the quantity grows 2 3/8 times, the total grows 2 3/8 times too.

Nova: So the letter x is just our name for Ahmes’ aha.

Lumi: Exactly. Using letters like x for unknowns became common much later, in the 1600s. The ideas are older than the symbols.

Now it is your turn: solve some aha problems like Ahmes, and then with today’s algebra.

¿Recuerdas a Ahmes, el escriba del pan? Algunos problemas de su papiro tratan de una cantidad desconocida que los egipcios llamaban aha, palabra que significa «cantidad» o «montón». El problema 24 dice: una cantidad y su séptima parte, sumadas, dan 19. ¿Cuál es la cantidad? Hoy escribiríamos x + x/7 = 19. Ahmes no usaba letras: usaba el método de la falsa posición. Probó a propósito con 7, porque su séptima parte es fácil: 7 + 1 = 8. Como necesitaba 19 y no 8, calculó 19 ÷ 8 = 2 3/8 y multiplicó su tanteo por ese número: 7 × 2 3/8 = 16 5/8. Lo comprobó: 16 5/8 + 2 3/8 = 19. Lumi explica que funciona porque la relación es proporcional, y que usar letras como x para las incógnitas se hizo común mucho después, en el siglo XVII.

The Rhind Papyrus has several aha problems. False position was still taught in arithmetic books in Europe many centuries later.

Part 1. Daniel is not right. The 3 multiplies everything inside the parentheses, so 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12, not 3x + 4. Then 3x + 12 = 27, so 3x = 15 and x = 5. Check: 3(5 + 4) = 3 × 9 = 27 ✓. (Another way: divide both sides by 3 first: x + 4 = 9, so x = 5.)

Challenge. With arithmetic: the last operation was “times 3,” so I undo it first: 27 ÷ 3 = 9. Then I undo “plus 4”: 9 − 4 = 5. The algebra steps “divide both sides by 3” and “subtract 4 from both sides” are the same operations in the same order.

Part 2. Salma is right. With the guess 4, the total is 4 + 1 = 5. The real total is 15, which is 15 ÷ 5 = 3 times bigger, so the quantity is 4 × 3 = 12. With algebra: x + x/4 = 15, which is the same as (5/4)x = 15, so x = 15 × 4/5 = 12. Check: 12 + 3 = 15 ✓.

Challenge. 15 + 3.50r ≤ 60, so 3.50r ≤ 45 and r ≤ 12.86. Rides come in whole numbers, so the greatest number of rides is 12 (12 rides cost $42, and $15 + $42 = $57).

The module opens with a true story from the history of mathematics: the aha problems of the Rhind Papyrus and Ahmes’ method of false position, which works because the relationship is proportional. At the Easy level the false position is guided step by step, the numbers are integers and division by a negative number does not appear; at the Challenge level the child uses decimal coefficients, solves aha problems with fraction answers, chooses the graph of an inequality and reverses the inequality sign when dividing by a negative number. A wrong answer always brings a hint, never the answer. The optional “Ayuda en español” button translates only the instruction or the question.
