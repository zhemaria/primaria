Can a law change π?

3,500 years ago, Ahmes found the area of a round field with a clever rule. In 1897, the lawmakers of Indiana, the state next to Illinois, almost passed a law with a wrong value of π. Lumi and Nova will tell you both stories. Then you will work with circles, angles, scale drawings, triangles, slices of solids, area and volume. In these games, use π ≈ 3.14, unless the problem asks for the answer in terms of π or tells you to use the π key on a calculator.

Ahmes’ round field and the Indiana Pi Bill

Ahmes’ round field. Problem 50 of the Rhind Papyrus asks for the area of a round field with a diameter of 9 khet. Ahmes’ rule: take away 1/9 of the diameter, which leaves 8, and multiply 8 × 8. The field has 64 setat of land. Ahmes replaced the circle with a square of side 8, and the two areas are almost the same. Today we can see that his rule works as if π were 256/81, about 3.16: less than one percent away from the true value, 3,500 years ago.

The Indiana Pi Bill. In 1897, Edward J. Goodwin, a country doctor and amateur mathematician, believed he had found how to “square the circle,” a problem that mathematicians already knew was impossible. A representative, Taylor I. Record, presented his idea to the Indiana House of Representatives as House Bill 246. The bill was so confusing that few lawmakers understood it, but on February 6, 1897, the House passed it 67 to 0. One of its claims would have made π equal to 3.2.

By chance, Professor C. A. Waldo, a mathematician from Purdue University, was at the Statehouse that week to ask for money for the Indiana Academy of Science. He was amazed to hear lawmakers debating mathematics, and he explained the errors. The Senate laughed at the bill and postponed it indefinitely. It never became law.

Nova: Wait. Ahmes was closer to the true π than the Indiana bill, more than 3,000 years earlier?

Lumi: Yes! 3.16 is much closer to 3.14159… than 3.2 is. And here is the big lesson: a vote can’t change mathematics. π is the same in Egypt, in Indiana and on the Moon.

Nova: So we can’t vote for π. We have to measure and prove.

π is the number of times the diameter fits around a circle. It never ends and never repeats, so we use an approximation, like 3.14 or the π key on a calculator. Sometimes we leave π as a symbol and write the exact answer “in terms of π,” like 25π. In these games, use π ≈ 3.14, unless the problem asks for something else.

El campo redondo de Ahmes. El problema 50 del papiro de Rhind pide el área de un campo redondo de 9 khet de diámetro. La regla de Ahmes: quitar 1/9 del diámetro, lo que deja 8, y multiplicar 8 × 8. El campo tiene 64 setat de tierra: Ahmes cambió el círculo por un cuadrado de lado 8, casi de la misma área. Su regla funciona como si π fuera 256/81, unos 3.16, a menos de un uno por ciento del valor verdadero. La ley de π de Indiana. En 1897, Edward J. Goodwin, un médico rural y matemático aficionado, creyó haber hallado cómo «cuadrar el círculo», algo que los matemáticos ya sabían que era imposible. El representante Taylor I. Record presentó su idea a la Cámara de Representantes de Indiana como el proyecto de ley 246, tan confuso que pocos lo entendieron; aun así, el 6 de febrero de 1897 la Cámara lo aprobó 67 a 0. Una de sus afirmaciones haría que π valiera 3.2. Por casualidad, el profesor C. A. Waldo, matemático de la Universidad Purdue, estaba esa semana en el Capitolio pidiendo fondos para la Academia de Ciencias de Indiana; se asombró al oír debatir matemáticas y explicó los errores. El Senado se burló del proyecto y lo aplazó indefinidamente: nunca fue ley. Nova se da cuenta de que Ahmes estuvo más cerca del π verdadero, y Lumi concluye: una votación no puede cambiar las matemáticas. Usamos aproximaciones de π, como 3.14 o la tecla π de la calculadora, o lo dejamos como símbolo para dar la respuesta exacta «en términos de π», como 25π. En estos juegos usa π ≈ 3.14, salvo que el problema pida otra cosa.

Both stories are told in the book Geometría Plana: Lo que debes saber, by José M. Fernández. A khet was an Egyptian unit of length, and a setat a unit of land area.

Part 1 · Explain

Find the area with π ≈ 3.14 and with Ahmes’ rule (take away 1/9 of the diameter and square what is left). Which one is closer to the true area?

El huerto escolar es un círculo de 10 pies de diámetro. Elías dice: «Su área es 80 pies cuadrados, porque π = 3.2. La Cámara de Indiana lo votó». ¿Tiene razón Elías? Halla el área con π ≈ 3.14 y con la regla de Ahmes (quitar 1/9 del diámetro y elevar al cuadrado lo que queda). ¿Cuál se acerca más al área verdadera?

Sentence starters: Elías is ___ because ___. The radius is ___ feet, so the area is 3.14 × ___ × ___ = ___. With Ahmes’ rule: 10 − 10/9 = ___, and ___ × ___ = ___.

Useful words: radius, diameter, area, π, approximation, closer, prove.

Challenge: the garden needs a fence around it. How many feet of fence are needed with π ≈ 3.14? And how many more feet would Elías buy with π = 3.2?

Reto: el huerto necesita una cerca alrededor. ¿Cuántos pies de cerca se necesitan con π ≈ 3.14? ¿Y cuántos pies de más compraría Elías con π = 3.2?

Part 2 · Apply the idea to a new problem

Two angles are supplementary. One of them is 20° more than three times the other. Athena says the angles are 40° and 140°. Is Athena right? Write an equation, solve it and check. Then write your own angle problem like this one.

Dos ángulos son suplementarios. Uno de ellos mide 20° más que el triple del otro. Athena dice que los ángulos miden 40° y 140°. ¿Tiene razón Athena? Escribe una ecuación, resuélvela y comprueba. Después escribe tu propio problema de ángulos parecido.

Sentence starters: Supplementary angles add up to ___°. If the smaller angle is x, the other is ___. My equation is x + ___ = 180. Then x = ___. Check: ___.

Challenge: on a map, 1 inch stands for 25 miles. Two towns are 4.5 inches apart on the map. How far apart are they really? If you redraw the map so that 1 inch stands for 50 miles, how far apart are they on the new map?

Reto: en un mapa, 1 pulgada representa 25 millas. Dos pueblos están a 4.5 pulgadas en el mapa. ¿A qué distancia están en realidad? Si vuelves a dibujar el mapa de modo que 1 pulgada represente 50 millas, ¿a qué distancia quedan en el mapa nuevo?

Part 1. Elías is not right. A vote cannot change π, and the Indiana bill never became law. The radius is 10 ÷ 2 = 5 feet, so the area is 3.14 × 5 × 5 = 78.5 square feet. With Ahmes’ rule: 10 − 10/9 = 8 8/9, and 8 8/9 × 8 8/9 ≈ 79.0 square feet. With 3.2: 3.2 × 25 = 80 square feet. The true area is about 78.54 square feet, so 3.14 is the closest, then Ahmes’ rule, and 3.2 is the farthest.

Challenge. Circumference = π × d = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 feet of fence. With 3.2: 3.2 × 10 = 32 feet, so Elías would buy 0.6 feet more than he needs.

Part 2. Athena is right. If the smaller angle is x, the other is 3x + 20. Supplementary angles add up to 180°: x + 3x + 20 = 180, so 4x = 160 and x = 40. The other angle is 3(40) + 20 = 140°. Check: 40 + 140 = 180 ✓.

Challenge. 4.5 × 25 = 112.5 miles. On the new map, 112.5 ÷ 50 = 2.25 inches (half of 4.5, because each inch now stands for twice as many miles).
