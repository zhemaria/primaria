Pascal, Fermat and the unfinished game

In 1654, a French nobleman who loved games of chance, the Chevalier de Méré, asked the young mathematician Blaise Pascal a puzzle that people had argued about for more than a century: two players put a prize in the middle and play until one of them wins a certain number of games. If they have to stop before the end, how should they share the prize fairly?

Pascal wrote letters about it to Pierre de Fermat, a lawyer from Toulouse who did mathematics as a hobby and was one of the best mathematicians of his time. In a letter of July 29, 1654, Pascal gives this example: each player puts in 32 gold coins (pistoles), so the prize is 64, and the first to win 3 games takes it all. They stop when the first player has 2 games and the other has 1.

Pascal thinks about the next game. If the first player wins it, he gets all 64 coins. If he loses, the score is 2 to 2, a tie, and each takes back 32. So the first player is sure of 32 coins either way. The other 32 depend on one game where both have the same chance, so they are shared in half: 16 and 16. Pascal’s fair share: 48 coins for the first player and 16 for the other.

Nova: Wouldn’t it be simpler to share by the games they won? 2 to 1 means 2/3 and 1/3.

Lumi: Long before Pascal, in 1494, a book by Luca Pacioli did exactly that! But it is not fair: what matters is not what already happened, but the chances of what could still happen. The first player needs only 1 more game; the other needs 2 in a row. That chance is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, so the fair shares are 3/4 and 1/4.

Nova: So Pascal and Fermat measured the future. That is probability!

Fermat solved the same puzzle in a different way, by listing every way the game could end, and the two methods agreed. Their letters are considered the beginning of the mathematics of probability.

En 1654, un noble francés aficionado a los juegos de azar, el caballero de Méré, le planteó al joven matemático Blaise Pascal un acertijo que llevaba más de un siglo discutiéndose: dos jugadores ponen un premio y juegan hasta que uno gane cierto número de partidas; si deben parar antes del final, ¿cómo se reparte el premio con justicia? Pascal le escribió sobre esto a Pierre de Fermat, un abogado de Toulouse que hacía matemáticas por afición. En una carta del 29 de julio de 1654, Pascal pone este ejemplo: cada jugador pone 32 monedas de oro (pistolas), el premio es 64 y gana quien llegue primero a 3 partidas; paran cuando el primero tiene 2 y el otro 1. Si el primero gana la siguiente, se lleva las 64; si la pierde, quedan 2 a 2 y cada uno recupera 32. Así que el primero tiene 32 seguras, y las otras 32 dependen de una partida con las mismas posibilidades para ambos: se reparten a la mitad. Reparto justo: 48 y 16. Nova propone repartir según las partidas ganadas (2/3 y 1/3), como hizo Luca Pacioli en 1494, pero Lumi explica que lo que cuenta son las posibilidades de lo que aún puede pasar: el segundo jugador necesita ganar 2 seguidas, con probabilidad 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Fermat lo resolvió de otra manera, enumerando todos los finales posibles, y los dos métodos coincidieron. Sus cartas se consideran el comienzo de la probabilidad.

A pistole was a gold coin used in France and Spain. Pascal was 31 years old when he wrote this letter.

Part 1. Daniel’s way is not fair. It shares by the rounds already played, like Pacioli in 1494, but what matters is the chance of winning from now on. Sophia wins the prize only if she wins the next 2 rounds: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Daniel wins in every other case: 3/4. So Daniel should get 3/4 × 24 = 18 stickers and Sophia 1/4 × 24 = 6 stickers. (Like Pascal: if Daniel wins the next round, he takes 24; if he loses, it is 2 to 2 and each takes 12. So 12 are sure for Daniel, and the other 12 are shared in half: 18 and 6.)

Challenge. At 2 to 0, Sophia needs to win 3 rounds in a row: 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8. Daniel’s chance is 7/8. Daniel: 7/8 × 24 = 21 stickers; Sophia: 3 stickers.

Part 2. Israel is not right. His three results are not equally likely. The sample space has 4 equally likely outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT. One heads and one tails happens in 2 of them (HT and TH), so the probability is 2/4 = 1/2. Even a famous French mathematician, Jean d’Alembert, made a mistake like this one in 1754!

Challenge. 18/60 = 0.3, so about 0.3 × 750 = 225 students would choose pizza. The sample must be random so that it represents the whole school; if we asked only the students in the pizza line, the result would be biased.
