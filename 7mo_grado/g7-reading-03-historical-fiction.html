The story: The Night the River Turned (historical fiction)

For eight years, my father came home from the canal with mud on his boots and dust in his hair. Mama said the canal ate our papá every morning and gave him back every night, a little more tired.

In January 1900, the newspapers said the river would soon run backward, away from the lake. My little brother laughed. “Rivers can't turn around,” he said. “They don't have feet.”

Papá said that men from Ireland, Sweden, Italy and Poland had dug side by side, and that half of them still couldn't say good morning to each other.

One freezing morning, Papá woke us before dawn. “Come,” he whispered. “Don't tell anyone. They are opening it today, before anyone can stop them.”

We stood on the bank with a few men in heavy coats, our breath making clouds. When the gate opened, the brown water hesitated, then slowly, slowly began to slide away from the lake.

Nobody cheered; it was too cold, and too strange.

Papá didn't say anything, but he took off his cap and held it against his chest. That was when I understood that the canal hadn't eaten my father; he had been building something that would keep thousands of children like us from getting sick.

What history says: Chicago’s waste flowed into Lake Michigan, where the city got its drinking water. From 1892 to 1900, thousands of workers dug the 28-mile Sanitary and Ship Canal. Because Missouri wanted a court to stop the project, Chicago opened the canal early, on January 2, 1900, with very few people watching, and the river began to flow away from the lake.

Part 1. The theme is that hard work that no one cheers for can still protect many people. At the beginning, Rosa repeats her mother’s words: “Mama said the canal ate our papá every morning and gave him back every night, a little more tired.” She sees the canal only as something that takes her father away. On the bank, the moment is quiet and strange: “Nobody cheered; it was too cold, and too strange.” Then her father’s silent gesture changes how she sees it: “he took off his cap and held it against his chest.” At the end, Rosa understands that “the canal hadn’t eaten my father; he had been building something that would keep thousands of children like us from getting sick.”

Challenge (summary). Rosa’s father dug the Chicago canal for eight years. One freezing morning in January 1900, he took his family to watch it open in secret. When the river began to flow away from the lake, Rosa understood that his work would protect children’s health.

Part 2. Two details from history are that the canal took about eight years to dig and that Chicago opened it early, in January 1900, before anyone could stop it. Two invented details are Rosa’s brother saying that rivers “don’t have feet” and Papá holding his cap against his chest. The author invented Rosa’s family because a history article can tell us when the canal opened and why, but a story can show how it felt to the people who built it and to their children.

Challenge. Because it is before dawn and freezing, it makes sense that only “a few men in heavy coats” are on the bank, just as history says. The cold and the darkness also make the scene quiet and secret, which fits the real reason the canal was opened early.

Aligned to the Illinois seventh grade English Language Arts standards RL.7.1 (cite several pieces of textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text), RL.7.2 (determine a theme or central idea of a text and analyze its development over the course of the text; provide an objective summary of the text), RL.7.3 (analyze how particular elements of a story or drama interact, e.g., how setting shapes the characters or plot), RL.7.4 (determine the meaning of words and phrases as they are used in a text, including figurative and connotative meanings; analyze the impact of rhymes and other repetitions of sounds, e.g., alliteration, on a specific verse or stanza of a poem or section of a story or drama), RL.7.5 (analyze how a drama's or poem's form or structure, e.g., soliloquy, sonnet, contributes to its meaning), RL.7.6 (analyze how an author develops and contrasts the points of view of different characters or narrators in a text) and RL.7.9 (compare and contrast a fictional portrayal of a time, place, or character and a historical account of the same period as a means of understanding how authors of fiction use or alter history). The five texts are historical fiction set in the times of the seventh grade math stories: three stories (ancient Egypt, France in 1654, Chicago in 1900), a poem about the red and black counting rods of ancient China and a play about the Indiana Pi Bill of 1897 with an aside and a line spoken to the audience. Every text has questions that separate the real history from the invented people and words. At the Easy level Lumi reads the text aloud while the sentence is highlighted, there are fewer options and the evidence is marked after a mistake; at the Challenge level the texts are longer and the student must prove each answer by choosing the sentence, line or stanza that supports it. Every wrong answer gets a hint, never the answer. The final task asks the student to trace how a theme develops with three exact quotations and to compare the story with a short historical account.
