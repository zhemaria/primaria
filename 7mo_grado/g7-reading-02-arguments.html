Final task: explain it yourself

There are no options to choose from this time. Judge an argument like an expert: is the evidence relevant and sufficient? Then write your own argument and answer the other side.

Esta vez no hay opciones para elegir. Juzga un argumento como un experto: ¿la evidencia es relevante y suficiente? Después escribe tu propio argumento y responde al otro lado.

The essay: Open the Library After School, by Valeria

1Every day at 3:15, the doors of our school library close, just when many students need it most. I believe our library should stay open until 5:00 on school days.

2First, many students have nowhere quiet to do homework. When our student council surveyed 150 students, 64 said they have no quiet place to study at home. The public library is more than a mile away, and many students can't get there alone.

3Second, an open library would help students finish their projects. Students could use the computers and printers, which many families don't have. Also, our library has new blue carpets and comfortable chairs.

4Some people might say that keeping the library open costs too much. That is a real concern, but parent volunteers and high school students who need service hours could help the librarian.

5My friend stayed after school once and got an A, so the library would raise everyone's grades. Let's open the doors to learning after 3:15!

Part 1 · Judge the argument

What is Valeria's claim? Find one piece of evidence that is relevant and sufficient, one detail that is not relevant to her reason, and one claim that is not supported enough. Quote all three and explain how you know.

¿Cuál es la afirmación de Valeria? Busca una evidencia relevante y suficiente, un detalle que no es relevante para su razón y una afirmación que no está suficientemente apoyada. Cita las tres y explica cómo lo sabes.

Sentence starters: Valeria's claim is ___. The evidence “___” is relevant and sufficient, because ___. The detail “___” is not relevant, because ___. The claim “___” is not supported enough, because ___.

Useful words: claim, reason, evidence, survey, relevant, irrelevant, sufficient, insufficient, counterclaim, position.

Challenge: explain how Valeria separates her position from the people who worry about the cost, and say whether her answer is convincing.

Reto: explica cómo separa Valeria su postura de la de quienes se preocupan por el costo, y di si su respuesta convence.

Part 2 · Your own argument

Write a short argument (six or seven sentences) about a change you would like at your school or in your neighborhood. Include a claim, two reasons, relevant evidence for each reason, and a fair description of the other side's position followed by your answer.

Escribe un argumento corto (seis o siete oraciones) sobre un cambio que te gustaría en tu escuela o en tu barrio. Incluye una afirmación, dos razones, evidencia relevante para cada razón y una descripción justa de la postura del otro lado, seguida de tu respuesta.

Sentence starters: I believe ___. The first reason is ___. For example, ___. The second reason is ___. In fact, ___. Some people believe ___, because ___. However, ___.

Challenge: name one more piece of evidence you would look for, and explain why it would be relevant and would make your argument sufficient.

Reto: nombra otra evidencia que buscarías y explica por qué sería relevante y haría suficiente tu argumento.

Part 1. Valeria’s claim is that the school library should stay open until 5:00 on school days. The evidence “When our student council surveyed 150 students, 64 said they have no quiet place to study at home” is relevant and sufficient for her first reason, because it comes from many students and is directly about having a quiet place to study. The detail “Also, our library has new blue carpets and comfortable chairs” is not relevant, because carpets and chairs don’t show that an open library helps students finish projects. The claim “My friend stayed after school once and got an A, so the library would raise everyone’s grades” is not supported enough, because one friend’s grade on one day can’t prove something about everyone.

Challenge. Valeria describes the other side fairly: she says the cost is “a real concern.” Then she separates her position by offering a solution, parent volunteers and high school students who need service hours. Her answer is partly convincing, but it would be stronger with evidence, such as the number of parents who are willing to help.

Part 2. “I believe our school should plant a vegetable garden. The first reason is that students learn science better by doing. For example, in our science unit on plants, our class grew beans in cups, and every student could explain how plants grow. The second reason is that a garden would give the cafeteria fresh food. In fact, a school in our neighborhood donated 200 pounds of vegetables to its kitchen last year. Some people believe a garden is too much work in the summer, because school is closed. However, families could sign up to water it one week each.”

Challenge. I would look for a survey of how many families would water the garden in the summer. It would be relevant because it answers the other side’s worry directly, and it would make my argument more sufficient because it shows that the plan can work.

Your words can be different. What matters is that you can judge whether evidence is relevant and sufficient, and that your argument treats the other side fairly.

Aligned to the Illinois seventh grade English Language Arts standards RI.7.6 (determine an author's point of view or purpose in a text and analyze how the author distinguishes his or her position from that of others), RI.7.8 (trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text, assessing whether the reasoning is sound and the evidence is relevant and sufficient to support the claims) and RI.7.9 (analyze how two or more authors writing about the same topic shape their presentations of key information by emphasizing different evidence or advancing different interpretations of facts). The three essays are written by seventh graders and include reasons, evidence, a counterclaim with an answer, evidence that is not sufficient and one unsupported claim on purpose. The two pairs connect with the seventh grade math stories: a report on the opening of the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal (1900) with an imagined letter from St. Louis, and an encyclopedia entry on the Indiana Pi Bill (1897) with an imagined diary of a lawmaker. The imagined texts are labeled as historical fiction, and one question teaches students to check their facts in other sources. At the Easy level there are fewer options and the sentence being studied is highlighted; at the Challenge level the student must prove the answer. Every wrong answer gets a hint, never the answer. The final task asks the student to judge whether the evidence in an essay is relevant and sufficient, and to write an argument that describes the other side fairly. The optional “Ayuda en español” button translates only the instruction or the question, never the texts or the answers, so the reading stays in English. Each game has its own direct link: #proposito, #afirmacion, #tipo, #apoyo, #evaluar, #comparar and #tarea.

Alineado con los estándares de Artes del Lenguaje en Inglés de Illinois para séptimo grado: RI.7.6 (determinar el punto de vista del autor y analizar cómo distingue su postura de la de otros), RI.7.8 (seguir y evaluar el argumento, juzgando si el razonamiento es sólido y si la evidencia es relevante y suficiente) y RI.7.9 (analizar cómo dos autores que escriben sobre el mismo tema presentan la información destacando evidencias distintas o interpretando los hechos de otra manera). Los textos imaginados se señalan como ficción histórica. Ante un error, siempre una pista, nunca la respuesta. Los juegos son en inglés; el botón «Ayuda en español» traduce solo la consigna o la pregunta.
